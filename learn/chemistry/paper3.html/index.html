How is the therapeutic index calculated for animals and humans?

Ratio between the therapeutic dose and the toxic/lethal dose

2. Aspirin and penicillin
Natural products with useful medicinal properties can be chemically altered to produce more potent or safer medicines.
What are the disadvantages of using natural medicines?

Aspirin
What is aspirin derived from?
Salicylic acid isolated from willow bark
caused severe digestive problems like stomach irritation, bleeding, and diarrhoea
How is it synthesised?
1. Salicylic acid reacts with ethanoic anhydride in condensation reaction
catalyst is concentrated phosphoric acid

2. Heated, diluted with water, cooled to form crystals
3. Impure product is recrystallised from hot ethanol
4. Aspirin confirmed by IR spectroscopy and melting point
How do mild analgesics like aspirin function?
Non-narcotic, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs
Intercept pain stimulus at source
By binding to enzyme cyclooyxgenase to suppress production of prostaglandins
Prostaglandins are responsible for fever, swelling and transmission of pain impulses from site of injury to brain
What are its five main uses?
Pain, fever, inflammation, blood clotting, prophylactic
1. Analgesic = relieve pain
2. Antipyretic = reduce fever
3. Anti-inflammatory - for irritation, infection, physical damage to cell tissues
4. Anticoagulant
Inhibiting cyclooxygenase prevents production of thromboxanes
Thromboxanes stimulate aggregation of platelets and blood clotting
5. Prophylactic = Reduce risk of stroke and heart attack
What are its four side effects?
1. Excessive bleeding and ulceration of stomach
risk of stomach bleeding increased with consumption of alcohol
2. Allergies
3. Acidosis - a decrease in pH due to salicylic acid
4. Reye’s syndrome in young children
How is its solubility and bioavailability increased?
Convert into ionic salt by neutralising carboxyl group with NaOH to produce sodium salt
Increases water solubility to facilitate distribution by bloodstream
In aqueous solution, sodium salt dissociates into sodium cations and acetylsalicylic acid anions to form ion-dipole and hydrogen bonds with water
However, effect limited as sodium salt converted back into aspirin when reacting with HCl in stomach
Why might paracetamol or ibuprofen be used instead?
No stomach bleeding/ulceration + fewer allergic reactions + doesn’t cause Reye’s syndrome
However, smaller therapeutic window + not anti-inflammatory + blood disorders + kidney/brain damage
Penicillin
What is penicillin?
Penicillins are class of antibiotics produced by fungi
Why is the beta-lactam ring important?
Key structural feature is responsible for anti-bacterial properties
Bond angles of 90° instead of 109.5° create ring strain
Amide group is very reactive
Binds to transpeptidase, responsible for bacterial cell wall cross-linking
How does penicillin function?
Beta-lactam ring opens and binds to enzyme transpeptidase
Responsible for cross-linking bacterial cell walls
Weakened cell walls leave cell vulnerable to osmotic pressure which will subsequently lyse
human/animal cells do not have cell ways so they are left unaffected
What are the consequences of over-prescribing penicillin? Why is patient compliance important?
Overprescription of penicillin or incomplete course by patients causes bacteria to develop antibiotic resistance
If course not completed, most resistant bacteria will survive, multiply and pass resistance to future generations
Bacteria mutate to producing enzyme penicillase which deactivates benzylpenicillin to prevent it from binding to transpeptidase
Over time, drug is less effective against common diseases
How do chemists deal with antibiotic resistance?
New penicillins with modified side-chains developed + “cocktail” of antibiotics
could not be deactivated by penicillase
more effective against wider variety of diseases
some more stable in acidic stomach environment (can be taken orally)

5. Antiviral medications
Antiviral medications have recently been developed for some viral infections while others are still being researched.
How do viruses differ from bacteria?
Lack cellular structure and do not have their own metabolism
Bacteria are larger living cells that can feed, grow, and multiply
What is the structure of a virus?
Viruses are nucleoproteins: nucleic acid + protein coat
What is the protein coating called?
Coat is called a capsid, consisting of multiple protein units (capsomeres) arranged in a helical or polyhedral structure
How do viruses work?
Can exist outside living organisms, cannot perform biological functions by themselves
What are the four stages?
Use metabolism of host cell to create copies
1. Capsid proteins bind to receptors on host cell surface
2. Virus uncoats, crosses the cell membrane and injects own genome into cell
3. Protein synthesis of viral components from hijacked DNA
4. Replicated viruses are released and cell is lysed
Why are viruses more difficult to target than bacteria?
Lack of cellular structure and metabolism
Have no cure, only treated symptomatically or immunisation
How do antiviral drugs work?
alter cell’s genetic material; inhibit virus entry/binding to cell; prevent virus from leaving cell; becomes part of virus DNA; prevent virus from replicating
How do second stage antivirals work?
amantadine and rimantadine inhibit uncoating of virus and injection of genome into cell
used against influenza and common cold
but all strains have developed resistance to both drugs
How do tertiary stage antivirals work?
Acyclovir and zidovudine mimic the structure of nucleotides
Used against herpes and HIV
In host cell, drugs undergo phosphorylation to produce non-standard nucleotides
These are then mistakenly incorporated into protein synthesis to deactivate enzymes
How do final stage antivirals like oseltamivir (Tamiflu) and zanamivir (Relenza) work as preventative agents against flu?
Prevent release of virus copies by inhibiting viral enzymes called neuraminidases
Neuraminidases trigger budding process which allows viruses to bulge through outer membrane of host cell
Inhibition keeps viruses trapped within cell to slow their spread around body
What is the difference between oseltamivir and zanamivir?
Oseltamivir has an ester group which makes it inactive in original form
In body, ester group hydrolysed into carbonyl group to produce active metabolite with enhanced antiviral activity
Zanamivir already has carboxyl group so is active in original form
Most stains are resistant to oseltamivir but not zanamivir
What is similar in both?
Both contain a six-membered ring with three chiral carbons

Progressive failure of immune system, development of life-threatening oppurtunistic infections and cancers

2. Destroys white blood cells responsible for fighting viral and bacterial infections
3. Cannot target HIV without damaging host cells
4. HIV can incorporate itself into host DNA to remain dormant for years

Retrovirus
Use reverse transcriptase enzymes to produce DNA from RNA in opposite process to protein synthesis where DNA -> RNA
Process only used by retrovirus: inhibition does not affect normal cells but reduces ability to multiply
Zidovudine can be used to combat AIDS / prevent HIV transmission
but cannot eliminate completely
used in combination with other reverse transcriptase inhibitors to slow development of resistance and increase efficiency of therapy

6. Environmental impact of some medicines
The synthesis, isolation, and administration of medications can have an effect on the environment.
What is the difference between high level waste (HLW) and low level waste (LLW)?
HLW emits large amounts of ionising radiation for long time
LLW emits small amounts of ionising radiation for short time
How does medical nuclear waste disposal affect the environment?
Nuclear Waste
How do we dispose of LLW?
LLW like contaminated syringes has limited environmental impact
some for shallow land burial or incineration
concentrated solutions of radionuclides must be stored for several days or weeks in shielded containers
then can be disposed of as normal when radioactivity levels are safe
HLW is produced in nuclear reactors and is a mixture of fission products + unused nuclear fuel
have long half-lives and/or highly active
How do we dispose of HLW?
1. ongoing nuclear reactions means water cooling is needed for several years
2. after low enough radioactivity level, HLW is vitrified
3. encased in steel cylinders, covered with concrete and buried deep underground in geologically stable formations
Problems: leak into water table, remain active for long time, released into environment during earthquakes, used by terrorists
What are the implications?
1. Ionising radiation causes cellular and genetic damage
2. Increases number of mutations, thus risk of cancer or birth defects
3. Weakens immune system by triggering apoptosis (programmed cell death)
4. Amplifies problem of antibiotic resistance by increasing mutation rate of bacteria, accelerating development of drug-resistant strains
Solvent Waste
How do left-over solvents relate to environmental issues?
Organic solvents are toxic to living organisms, affecting nervous and respiratory system
Benzene and chloroform increase risk of cancer
Highly flammable while vapours contribute to greenhouse effect
Chlorinated solvents are ozone-depleting agents and contribute to formation of photochemical smog in cities
are not biodegradable and accumulate in groundwater
disposal requires separated oxidation at high temperatures or distillation recycling otherwise toxic phosgene released
instead use supercritical carbon dioxide
Antibiotic Waste
How do micro-organisms develop antibiotic resistance?
over-prescription of antibacterials, patient non-compliance in finishing course, antibacterials in agriculture, release of waste by hospitals
bacteria like MRSA can survive and mutate to develop resistance to drugs
pass down resistance to new generations
Why is improper drug disposal and animal waste dangerous?
Antibiotics are never completely metabolised in animals so faeces contain original antibiotics which are eventually consumed by us
Green Chemistry
What are the key concepts of green chemistry processes?
Maximum amount of all raw materials ends up in the product with a minimum amount of waste generated
1. Less hazardous feedstock
2. Milder reaction conditions, better catalysts and less hazardous solvents
substitute hazardous solvents for those with better environmental health + safety
substitute organic solvents for environmentally harmless
use ionic liquids with low vapour pressure
3. Production methods with fewer steps and higher atom economy
What are the benefits of using supercritical fluids?
effective solvents with low viscosities, high diffusion rates
CO2 has low critical point, cheap, easily removed and non-toxic

What is Taxol?
Taxol
How is taxol used as a chemotherapeutic agent?
Destroys cancerous cells by binding to tubulin proteins and interfering with cell division
Where does it naturally occur?
Pacific yew tree
but high extraction cost, low yield, limited yew bark

How is taxol obtained now?

How do chiral auxiliaries form a desired enantiomer?
Chiral Auxillaries
Multiple chiral centres mean a racemic mixture of several stereoisomers is produced
Chiral auxiliaries are added to the starting material and removed when synthesis is complete
A* = chiral auxiliaries, S=substrate, P=product
1. Auxiliary A* + non-chiral substrate S form chiral intermediate S-A*
2. The configuration of the existing chiral centre in auxiliary forces the reaction to form only one of two possible diastereomers P-A*
3. Auxiliary is removed, producing product P
Recycling auxiliary reduces economic and environmental cost
Specific stereoisomers are synthesised but unwanted diastereomers can be formed
have different physical and chemical properties
separated through crystallisation, extraction, or chromatography
Explain the role of the chiral auxiliary in the synthesis of Taxol
1. Chiral auxiliary is optically active
2. Attaches to non-chiral substrate
3. Creates stereochemical conditions necessary to follow a certain pathway
4. Allows only the required enantiomer to form
How is a polarimeter used to identify enantiomers?
Polarimeter
Measures the angle of rotation of plane-polarised light caused by optically active molecules
Two enantiomers rotate light in opposite directions equally

What are the two main types of radiotherapy?
Radiotherapy
Which types of emissions are used for medical treatment?
Alpha, beta, gamma, proton, neutron, positron
1. How does Targeted Alpha Therapy (TAT) treat dispersed diseases?
Treats leukaemia and other cancers that have spread throughout body
Alpha emitters like Pb-212 are delivered by carrier drug/protein directly to targeted cancer cells
Cancer cells are selectively destroyed by radiation without much damage to surrounding tissue
Collision of 𝜶 and 𝜷 with nuclei produce secondary gamma radiation, used to map distribution of cancer in body

2. How does Boron Neutron Capture Therapy (BNCT) work?
High-intensity neutron beams where neutrons are absorbed by boron-10
After “capturing” a neutron, B-10 transforms into B-11, which then undergoes alpha decay to form lithium
BNCT
Causes extensive cellular damage within limited range of 0.005-0.01 mm (size of a cell)
Administered via BPA, similar to amino acids, accumulates in all growing tissue including tumours

Raoult's Law
